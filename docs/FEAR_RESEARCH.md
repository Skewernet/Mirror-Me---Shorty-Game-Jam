# Fear research and original creature direction

Working research brief, 23 October 2026. Full story spoilers. This is guidance for the local post-0.7 creature revision, not a claim that every recommendation has shipped.

## What this brief can and cannot establish

There is no defensible universal ranking of the “scariest monster.” Culture, prior exposure, personal associations, sensory preferences and the context of a scene change the response. A creature that looks impressive in a bright render can be ineffective in a game; a nearly ordinary person can become frightening through timing, context and apparent intention.

This brief distinguishes three things:

- **Published literature:** the work listed below addresses anticipation, threat, uncanny action, alarm sounds and recreational horror. Its scope is narrower than this game.
- **Film interpretation:** the film comparisons below are our reading of dramatic techniques. They are not audience experiments or quotations from the filmmakers.
- **Design hypotheses:** the recommendations are ideas to implement and test in MIRROR ME. Research does not guarantee that a particular jaw shape, exposure duration or sound will frighten a player.

**Retrieval limitation:** attempts in this environment to reach PubMed/PMC, Europe PMC, Crossref, OpenAlex, Semantic Scholar, DOI/publisher sites and film-reference sites were rejected by the network proxy with HTTP 403 on 23 October. Consequently the bibliography is a literature-informed starting list of known publications, not a newly completed full-text review. Links, bibliographic details and interpretations should be checked against the publications when access is available. No quotations, exact effect sizes, participant counts or numerical “fear rankings” are claimed here.

## Useful evidence, with practical limits

| Topic | Literature-informed finding | Design inference for MIRROR ME | Limit |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Anticipation and uncertainty | Work on anxiety treats uncertainty about possible future threat as consequential; anticipation can matter before contact occurs. [1] | Let the player hear or glimpse something that has an intelligible location but an uncertain intention. A still body that might move is useful before its first movement. | Clinical/neuroscience models are not a recipe for entertaining horror. Randomness alone does not create meaning. |
| Threat proximity | Experimental work distinguishes responses as an apparent threat becomes more imminent. [2] | Establish a distant, recognizable body; reserve intimate proximity for a rare, authored moment. The transition should be readable. | Scanner tasks and virtual threats differ from a domestic horror game. It does not justify trapping the player or a mandatory chase. |
| Human appearance and unexpected action | Uncanny-action research investigates the mismatch between an agent's appearance and expected movement. [3] | Keep enough human anatomy for the player to predict a movement, then violate one expectation: the head remains oriented as the body withdraws, or a forearm takes weight in a way it should not. | This is one line of work, not proof that all humanlike models or all mismatches are frightening. “Uncanny valley” is not a synonym for poor animation. |
| Alarm-sound texture | Research on human screams examines acoustic roughness as part of an alarm communication signal. [4] | A brief, controlled rough throat or breath accent can mark an impossible articulation. Build contrast with quiet and localizable ordinary breathing. | Roughness is not loudness. The research does not prescribe an amplitude, prove a game cue works or justify painful/high-volume playback. |
| Recreational fear | Field research on recreational horror examines relationships between fear/arousal and enjoyment rather than treating maximum arousal as maximum enjoyment. [5] | Give tension room to rise and release. Leave safe intervals for reading, puzzle thought and story recognition. | Visitors to a horror attraction are a selected audience; their reactions cannot predict this game's players. |
| Horror response varies | A broad review describes the range of individual and contextual factors studied in responses to horror films. [6] | Test with different players. Keep captions and existing settings useful; do not define success only as a jump or a shout. | Film evidence does not directly transfer to interactive pacing, and a review is not an endorsement of our asset design. |

The strongest practical conclusion is to design an encounter as a sequence of expectations, rather than keep increasing deformation, blood or volume.

## What the film references suggest

### The Exorcist: an ordinary room becomes an intolerable place

Our reading is that its force comes partly from the familiar and vulnerable becoming untrustworthy: a home, a bed, a recognizable person, caretaking, a voice and a body whose behavior no longer agrees with its identity. The bodily violation matters because the ordinary person and ordinary room were established first. Physical detail, seriousness and restraint around the impossible make the violation feel consequential.

For MIRROR ME, retain the bedroom, door frames, realistic domestic lighting and Mara's recognizable human scale. Let one articulation or one sustained look break the expected rules. Do not borrow Regan's face, makeup, voice, levitation, head-turn set piece or religious iconography. A recognizable imitation will shrink this game's own identity.

### Incantation: apparent reassurance changes meaning

Our reading is that its participatory framing and the reinterpretation of protective language make attention feel implicated. Information offered as help acquires another meaning. Its effect is not reducible to the final image alone; the surrounding belief, forbidden knowledge and delayed reinterpretation prepare that image.

For MIRROR ME, the strongest parallel is already in the script: Mara's care routine and “good daughter” account change meaning when the same house is seen truthfully. The mirror should keep looking at the hands and person she omits. Do not copy the film's face, symbol, chant or viewer-directed curse. Do not secretly change puzzle rules or frame the player's ordinary controls as consent to supernatural harm.

The game's specific emotional threat is: **the familiar person looking back knows what these hands did.** Its monster should reinforce that sentence.

### Comparing monster and threat concepts

This is a small comparative reading, **not a ranking of the scariest films or a claim about what frightens most viewers**. The concepts below are useful for different reasons; combining every one would dilute the game. Each transfer is an original design hypothesis to test.

| Reference / concept | Critical interpretation | Useful original transfer | Reject for MIRROR ME |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| *The Exorcist* (1973) / the familiar person violated | Human identity, bodily agency and ordinary care cease to agree inside a credible bedroom. | Keep recognizable human proportions and one disturbing contradiction between face, voice or action. | Regan's likeness or signature set pieces; possession as an explanation that excuses Mara. |
| *Incantation* (2022) / reassurance becomes implication | Protective-seeming information is reinterpreted; attention and participation become part of the dramatic threat. | Let the same care routine and mirror acquire a harsher meaning after the evidence is known. | Its chant, symbol, final face or curse mythology; secretly dishonest puzzle rules. |
| *The Thing* (1982) / body and identity become unreliable | A recognizable body may conceal something incompatible with its appearance; transformation makes mistrust physical. | Establish an ordinary anatomical expectation before one specific tendon, mouth or weight-bearing violation. | Copying its transformations, a mutation showcase or a hidden alien culprit. Mara's hands must remain her own. |
| *The Blair Witch Project* (1999) / unseen intention | Partial signs and uncertain location leave the threat's intention unresolved; absence does dramatic work. | A finite, localizable room sound and a trace of movement can prepare a later sighting without showing the whole body. | Endless wandering, illegible objectives or arbitrary ambient noises with no spatial relationship to the house. |
| *Hereditary* (2018) / domestic negative space and ceiling presence | A familiar room can contain an alarming figure before attention locates it; the vertical space alters the reading of home. | Compose a real ceiling silhouette against readable architecture, then allow one deliberate orientation toward the player. | Recreating an iconic pose or sequence; hiding every encounter in near-blackness or turning the family tragedy into an interchangeable cult plot. |
| *Ju-on: The Grudge* (2002) / familiar human form with wrong locomotion | Crawling, obstructed facial recognition and abnormal vocal texture disturb the expected relation between a person and a movement. | Give the creature a distinctive weight transfer, sustained gaze and restrained throat/breath identity that belong to this body. | The stock long-haired white-gown ghost, borrowed vocal signature or a crawl with no convincing contacts. |
| *Alien* (1979) / an intelligible predator in constrained space | Partial exposure and a consistent silhouette support a threat that appears to occupy the same physical environment as its victim. | Respect door width, light, occlusion and a coherent body silhouette; let the architecture support discovery. | Turning the encounter into a patrol, health-bar enemy or combat/chase grammar. That changes the question from what Mara has done to whether the player can defeat a creature. |

The best fit is the combination of **recognizable human identity, uncertain intention and domestic space that no longer offers reassurance**. Body violation is punctuation within that direction. It should not replace the story with a catalogue of spectacular monsters.

## Proposed original creature: the witness in the wrong posture

“The witness” is a working direction, not a new external murderer or a required on-screen name. It is a bodily manifestation of Mara's evasive account. Its human resemblance should become more unsettling as the player recognizes it.

### Anatomy and material

1. **A human silhouette with a coherent violation.** Preserve the scale of shoulders, hands and head relative to the actual house. Choose a dominant deformity that reads from the hallway: a collapsed neck and fixed gaze above an improperly weight-bearing torso, for example. Avoid an indiscriminate collection of horns, spikes, giant teeth, six limbs and glowing eyes.
2. **Face before decoration.** At normal encounter distance the eye sockets, head angle and jaw should read. A wet lower eyelid, recessed mouth and skin stretched over a tense jaw are more useful than an unreadable high-frequency texture. The face must remain comprehensible without a flashlight aimed perfectly at it.
3. **Skin has structure.** Use restrained variations in translucency, pallor, bruise/pressure areas, dry skin and localized wetness. Roughness and lighting should describe tissue, not make the entire body glossy plastic. Keep damage asymmetrical and physically related to load or strain.
4. **Hands carry the story.** Hands and forearms can carry the strongest original blood/evidence treatment. The player's truthful final mirror still needs recognizable Mara with bloodied hands; it must not imply a separate demon committed the murder.
5. **Aging and illness are not monster shorthand.** Evelyn's illness is context and her death is a crime. Do not turn her sick body into the evil that needed defeating. Avoid conflating disability, caregiving need or an ordinary older woman's face with moral corruption.

### Movement grammar

- **Real contact, impossible intention.** Palms and toes plant, mass transfers, and the torso responds. Feet and hands should not skate while the model rotates. The impossible detail should be deliberate, not a rig failure.
- **Stillness is an action.** Begin selected sightings with an uncomfortable hold, not an idle sway loop. A single slow head reorientation can communicate awareness before locomotion starts.
- **One broken expectation at a time.** A backward withdrawal with the face still attending to the player is enough. Pairing a fast spin, jitter, jaw oscillation and random limb flail will obscure the event.
- **Different exposures have different purposes.** A ceiling presence can be quiet and nearly stationary. The distant hallway body can hold, orient, then withdraw with weight. The final witness can be slower and accusatory. Reusing the same crawl speed and cue everywhere turns the creature into a familiar animation.
- **No demonstration reel.** Do not hold the creature in neutral, well-lit full view long enough for the player to inspect every joint unless that sustained inspection is the point of that particular scene.

### Encounter grammar

An encounter can use **suggestion → recognition → one violation → absence**. This is a working composition, not a scientifically established formula.

- The suggestion has a plausible source: a breath in a room, a pressure sound above the door, a hand briefly visible beyond a frame.
- Recognition gives the player enough information to understand a body occupies the real house. Use actual sightlines and occlusion, not an unexplained screen overlay.
- The violation is a finite, specific event. Avoid multiple stacked loud events competing with a crucial clue or voice line.
- Absence lets the player reconsider what they saw. Withdrawal should disappear behind real architecture or into a room, without visible clipping or a slow transparent dissolve in open space.

Sound may give a nearby player an optional reason to look. A creature should not consume its only meaningful sighting while completely hidden behind a closed door. Looking away must not secretly fail a puzzle, lock progress or repeatedly punish the player with a new jump.

## Sound, accessibility and habituation

The user wants strong fear, but continuous maximum stimulation usually destroys the contrast that lets a scene work. Give each cue a start, a location and an end. A small pressure change, strained inhalation or fabric drag can precede one short rough accent; constant yelling teaches the player to ignore the mix.

Keep dialogue, the 93.7 clue, clock clue, keepsake order and final report intelligible. Do not make a scare depend on a cue audible only in headphones. A player who mutes voices, uses captions or disables sudden scares must still receive the story and retain control. Preserve the existing reduced-scare path and finite light disturbances; do not add rapid persistent flicker as a substitute for animation or staging.

Use variation deliberately rather than randomizing every element. Familiar movement, repeated identical sound and predictable timing can make a threatening presence routine. Conversely, completely arbitrary cues can seem disconnected from a body or place. The useful design target is a small set of intelligible behaviors whose timing and intent are uncertain.

## How to judge this revision

Before publication, inspect the asset in the actual house at gameplay distance and normal lighting, with bloom/flashlight assumptions stated. A bright turntable is a modeling aid, not proof that the encounter works.

Technical checks should preserve planted contacts, doorway clearance, ceiling bounds, real occlusion, puzzle gates, reset, pause, mute and reduced-scare behavior. The graphics should remain readable at the house's existing dark/light balance; do not fix an unconvincing model by making the kitchen door invisible again.

A small human playtest should ask, after play rather than during the reveal:

- What did you think the creature was, and what did it want?
- What made you hesitate: its face, its movement, a sound, anticipation or uncertainty about the room?
- Did it seem to cause Mara's crime? If yes, the direction conflicts with the story.
- Was any encounter funny, repetitive or obviously a rig/lighting glitch?
- Could you understand the next puzzle and the final report?
- Which moments felt too intense, too quiet or inaccessible?

Do not convert a few responses into a claim about what “most people” fear. A calm player who starts checking doorways may be engaged; a loud startled reaction is not the only sign of success. Record observations and revise the least convincing moment first.
